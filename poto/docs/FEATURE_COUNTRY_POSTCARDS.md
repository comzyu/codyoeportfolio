# Poto: country postcards

Written 18 Nov 2026 from Cody's brief. One Poto postcard, 30 country editions. The photo and Poto's identity never change; each country adds a few small, real details from its own postcard tradition, pressed into the paper as **3D embossing**, with colour used sparingly. You can only get a country's edition by **being there** or **receiving one from someone there**, so they're rare, collectable, and a reason to send postcards across borders.

---

## 1. The idea in one line

Your postcard looks different depending on where it comes from, and you can't fake where it comes from.

## 2. How you get them (the rules)

| How | What you get | Mark on the card |
|---|---|---|
| **Your home country** | Your country's edition, always (set from your time zone and region at sign-up) | none: it's your everyday postcard |
| **You shoot it there** (the Poto camera's location confirms you're in that country) | That country's **local edition** for every postcard you send while you're there | a small **"shot in"** mark: *shot in Kyoto · 17 Nov* |
| **Someone sends you one** (a friend sends you a postcard from their country, or while travelling) | That country's edition, **received** | a **postmark** with their city and date, plus their signature stamp: *received from Dana · Osaka · 17 Nov* |

- A **received** edition and a **shot-in** edition of the same country look different (the postmark versus the "shot in" mark), so collectors know which is which. Receiving one is the rarer kind for most people.
- You **can't buy** a country edition. That's what keeps them meaningful (Cody's rule: no shortcuts to authenticity, as with no importing photos).
- **Proof:** the location comes from the Poto camera at the moment of shooting, city-level, same as Travel rolls. No camera-roll photos exist in Poto, so there's nothing to spoof with old photos.
- **Location turned off:** you only get your home edition and ones you receive.

## 3. The collection

- **A passport on your desk:** a small booklet object showing the 30 countries. Each one has two slots, **shot in** and **received**, with the date and who sent it. Empty slots are blind-embossed outlines, so you can feel what's missing.
- **First of a country:** the first time you receive one, it arrives with a slightly longer reveal (the emboss catching the light as the card turns).
- **On your desk:** country postcards display like any postcard, and their emboss reads in the desk's light.
- **Shareable:** "my passport" as a 9:16 / 4:5 image, the countries you've collected in a grid of embossed tiles. This is the travel brag people post at the end of a year.

## 4. Why it spreads

- **You want a friend abroad to send you one,** so you ask them to join.
- **Travelling makes you send postcards home,** because your friends can't get that edition any other way. Every trip is a burst of postcards from a new country.
- **Diaspora and international friend groups** (huge on Instagram and TikTok) suddenly have something to trade.
- **The passport share** is the year-end flex, like Spotify Wrapped for travel.

## 5. What changes per country, and what never does

**Never changes (Poto's identity)**
- The photo: clean, true colour, nothing on it (Cody's rule).
- The proportions, the white border, the matte paper, the poto mark on the frame.
- The sender's signature stamp and handwritten note.

**Changes per country** (small, and real: taken from that country's postcard tradition)
1. **The word "postcard"** printed on the back in that country's language and script. The single strongest signal, and it's how real postcards have always differed.
2. **The back layout quirk:**
   - East Asian postal-code boxes (Japan, Korea, Taiwan)
   - right-to-left layout (Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Pakistan)
   - the classic French "Correspondance / Adresse" labels
   - the bilingual header (Canada, India, South Africa, Singapore)
3. **One blind-embossed motif** from that country's craft or architecture: a pattern pressed into the paper, no ink, felt more than seen.
   - **On the front:** only on the white border, very shallow, so the photo stays the hero.
   - **On the back:** deeper, as a frame, corner pieces or a full field behind the message.
4. **One accent ink:** used for the postcard word and the dividing line on the back, never on the front border.
5. **A postmark style** for "received" editions: round, wavy-line or framed, matching that country's postmark tradition.

## 6. The embossing (how it looks and how we build it)

- **Blind emboss** (raised, no colour) is the default. Some countries get a **deboss** (pressed in) where that's the craft's character, e.g. papercut patterns read better pressed in.
- **In the 3D postcard:** the emboss is a height map, the same system the paper texture and typewriter dents already use. So it catches the light as you turn the card, which is the whole point, and costs almost nothing to render.
- **In the flat share images:** the emboss is baked in with light from the top left, the same as the 3D view's default.
- **Strength:**
  - front border at about 20% of the back's depth
  - back motif deep enough to read on a phone screen at arm's length
  - always checked in light and dark mode
- **One motif system:** every country's motif is drawn as clean vector line-work at the same weight, so the 30 feel like one family. They're Poto's interpretations, never copies of a specific artwork.

## 7. Respect, rights and care (important)

- **No official marks:**
  - no postal operator logos (Royal Mail, La Poste, USPS, Japan Post's 〒 as a logo, etc.)
  - no national flags or coats of arms
  - no royal symbols

  Many are trademarks or legally protected. The local word for "postcard" and generic layouts are fine.
- **Indigenous and sacred art:** don't use designs that belong to a specific community (for example Aboriginal Australian art, or patterns tied to religious meaning). Where a pattern is a living craft (batik, adire, Ndebele, wayuu weaving), our motif is a respectful, generic geometric nod. Ideally we check each one with someone from that country before launch, and credit the craft in the passport ("pattern after Indonesian kawung batik").
- **Script accuracy:** every non-Latin word must be checked by a native speaker before shipping. The words below are my best knowledge, not verified.

## 10. Build notes

- **Paper:**
  - every country edition is a variation of the one postcard renderer: a word, a layout flag, a motif height map and an accent colour
  - a new country is a data entry plus one motif drawing
- **Motif drawings:** 30 vector motifs drawn to one line weight, exported as height maps. This is the main design work.
- **Location:** country detection from the camera's GPS at the moment of the shot (no network lookup needed); cached so offline shots still count.
- **Server:** the edition is decided when the shot is taken and stored with the postcard, so it can't be changed later.

## 11. Money (light touch)

Country editions are **never sold**; that's the point. They support the paid side indirectly:
- **Plus:** the passport's full-resolution share image; a "send from your country" desk display.
- **Shop:** passport covers and desk display cases, as cosmetic items.
- **City and seasonal editions** (later), earned by being there, or as limited event editions for Poto+ Events.
